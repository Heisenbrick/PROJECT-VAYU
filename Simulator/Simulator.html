The Physics of Gyroscopic Shear

When you attempt to tilt a spinning rotor, it strongly resists changing its axis of rotation due to Gyroscopic Precession. Instead of tilting cleanly, a standard gyroscope will react by applying a massive 90-degree off-axis torque. For a heavy-lift drone transitioning to forward flight, this torque is strong enough to rip the carbon-fiber wing spars clean off the fuselage.

τ_gyro = I * Ω_rotor × Ω_tilt

The torque is proportional to the rotational inertia of the blades (I), the spinning speed of the rotor (Ω_rotor), and how fast you try to tilt the nacelle (Ω_tilt).

The Dual Coaxial-Duct Solution

To safely tilt the rotors without snapping the wings, our drone uses a clever mechanical trick. By mounting two counter-rotating props (one spinning Clockwise, one spinning Counter-Clockwise) inside each duct, we create two equal but opposite gyroscopic torques during transition.

As the nacelle tilts forward, the top rotor tries to twist the wing UP, while the bottom rotor tries to twist the wing DOWN. Because both rotors are mounted on the same reinforced titanium spar within the duct, these violent twisting forces fight each other and cancel out.

The result? The net torque transferred back to the delicate aircraft fuselage is exactly zero, allowing a smooth, rapid transition from hover to cruise.
